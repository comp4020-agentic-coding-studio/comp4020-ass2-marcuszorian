# The leakage taxonomy

Six weeks, six channels, six disclosures. This lecture is the first time the
course asks you to compare them instead of adding a seventh.

---

## The six axes

- physical medium (sound, current, EM, cache timing, light, heat)
- attacker proximity required
- available during computation, or only after
- information per measurement, versus measurements required
- cost to close
- (new, this week) bits of the secret actually recoverable

---

{/* _class: centered */}

## "It leaks" is not a number

Every channel covered so far was shown to leak *something*. None of those
demonstrations said *how much*, in bits, under realistic noise. That gap is
what this lecture closes.

---

## Mutual information, briefly

For a secret `S` and a measurement `M` of it:

```
I(S; M) = H(S) - H(S | M)
```

How much uncertainty about `S` remains, on average, once you know `M`. Zero
means the channel told you nothing. `H(S)` means it told you everything.

---

## Placing channels on the taxonomy

| Channel | Proximity | Available | Bits / trace |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acoustic (wk 2) | same room | during | low, needs many |
| Power (wk 3) | physical contact | during | high, few traces |
| Cache (wk 6) | shared core | during | medium |
| Thermal (wk 8) | none, after the fact | after only | low, decays with time |

Fill in the rest from your own Bench notes. There is no single correct
ranking — that is what the Bench and the capstone both test.

# A number a defender can act on

"This channel leaks 0.2 bits per trace and needs physical access" and "this
channel leaks 6 bits per trace over the network" are not the same
engineering problem, even though both are, technically, side channels.
