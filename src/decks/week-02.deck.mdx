# Sixty keystrokes

You typed a passage you had already written down, and recorded yourself
doing it. What is in the recording that is not in the passage?

Sixteen bursts, not eight. Before anything is labelled, that is the only
fact on the slide worth having.

```notes
Amplitude envelope of a phone recording, eight characters typed at a
comfortable pace. Sampling is irrelevant here — everything on this slide
survives at 16 kHz.

Every keystroke is two events, and the fifth is louder than its neighbours
by about 4 dB.
```

---

## One keystroke is two events

The finger drives the key down onto the plate; the key returns and strikes
its stop. Push, then release, about 100 ms apart.

The push is the informative one. It carries the plate's resonance at the
position the key sits in, which is why position is recoverable at all.

## What separates by hand, and what does not

Size and position separate: the space bar, Enter, the keys nearest the
recorder. Four bursts of a two-key alternation separate.

Individual letters do not. That is the boundary this Bench is meant to run
into, because it is where the 2004 result stopped and the next one started.

---

## The decisive move was not the microphone

Asonov and Agrawal trained on the same keyboard they attacked. The
improvement came from dropping that requirement and using the language
instead: spelling, word frequency, letter transitions.

A classifier that is unsure between two keys is resolved by the sentence, not
by a better recording. The channel became a language problem.

---

## Then the keyboard stopped being necessary

Genkin, Shamir and Tromer recorded the laptop rather than what was typed on
it. Coils and capacitors in the voltage regulator vibrate as the processor's
load changes.

| Quantity | Genkin, Shamir and Tromer, 2014 |
|---|---|
| Band carrying the signal | under 20 kHz |
| Sensor | a phone beside the machine, or a good mic at 4 m |
| Recovered | a full 4096-bit RSA key, in about an hour |

---

## Five orders of magnitude below the clock

The channel never sees an operation. It sees the envelope of very many of
them, and the key falls out of the aggregate.

Which is the honest answer to how far the effect generalises: not "any
keyboard" but "any component whose current draw makes a noise."

# The fix was arithmetic

Nobody shielded anything. GnuPG 1.4.16 shipped exponentiation blinding
alongside the disclosure — randomise the intermediate values, and a chosen
ciphertext no longer produces a profile worth measuring.

An acoustic leak, closed in software.

---

## Before the Bench

Record a known passage on one keyboard — phone audio is enough — and keep
the text. Sixty keystrokes is the floor.

The Bench asks you to align the waveform against the passage by hand, point
at two keys that are visibly different, and state what would have to be true
of a second keyboard for the technique to fail.
