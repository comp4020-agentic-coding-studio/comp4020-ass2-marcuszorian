# Six moves

Seven channels are real. A defender has budget for two of them. Which two,
and how would you defend the choice to somebody who disagrees?

---

## The method

1. State the device.
2. State the adversary — proximity, dwell time, budget.
3. Rule out the channels that adversary cannot reach.
4. Rank what survives: rate against cost to close.
5. Name what you are choosing not to defend.
6. Say what would change your mind.

---

## Move 1 — the device

A battery-powered hotel door lock. Four-digit PIN on a plastic membrane
keypad, code rotated per stay, verified on a microcontroller inside the
door, no network.

Housing is screwed shut and its screws are inside the room.

---

## Move 2 — the adversary

The guest in the next room. Corridor access for a few minutes at a time, a
phone, no tools. They cannot open the housing, cannot run code on the
microcontroller, and will not stand there for an hour.

Everything below follows from those four sentences.

---

## Move 3 — ruling out

| Channel | Reachable? | Because |
|---|---|---|
| Power (wk 3) | no | needs contact with the board, housing is shut |
| Cache (wk 6) | no | needs code running on the same processor |
| EM (wk 5) | no | needs a receiver and dwell time the corridor denies |

Three of seven are gone, and none of them were ruled out by a
countermeasure. The adversary statement did it.

---

## Move 4 — ranking what survives

| Channel | Rate | Cost to close |
|---|---|---|
| Thermal (wk 8) | set of digits, order decays in ~60 s | brushed metal keypad, at manufacture |
| Acoustic (wk 2) | per-key, needs a quiet corridor | silent membrane, cheap |
| Optical (wk 7) | full PIN, needs line of sight | recessed keypad, cheap |
| Timing (wk 4) | none — no remote interface to time | already closed |

---

## The ranking is not the rate column

Thermal leaks less than optical and is first anyway, because the fix is a
purchasing decision made once, years before the attack, and cannot be
retrofitted to an installed lock.

Cheap to exploit, expensive to close. That is week 10's divergence, on one
device.

---

## Move 5 — what is not defended

An adversary who opens the housing gets the power channel and this design
does not stop them. Stated, not hidden: the lock assumes the corridor, not
the workshop.

A design review that admits no residual risk has not finished.

---

## Move 6 — what would change your mind

- If the PIN check moves to Bluetooth, timing returns to the top of the list.
- If the keypad ships in brushed metal, thermal leaves it.
- If the corridor gets a camera, optical is somebody else's problem already.

Each is a fact about the device, not an opinion about the ranking.

---

## Two ways this goes wrong

**An adversary stated so loosely every channel applies.** "A determined
attacker" rules nothing out, so move 3 does no work and the ranking is
seven rows of guesswork.

**A ranking with no residual risk admitted.** Move 5 is the one people skip.

---

## Where the method stops

Week 9: the cheapest defence is often not on the channel list at all.
Rotating the code per stay already caps what any of these channels is worth
— it is the lock's colocation defence, and it defends all four at once.

Look for that move before ranking. Sometimes it is the whole answer.

# Real is not the same question as worth defending

The second one is what an engineer is paid to answer, and it needs a device
and an adversary before it means anything.

This Bench runs the six moves on your draft, out loud, against one sceptical
peer — before the capstone runs them against a marker.
